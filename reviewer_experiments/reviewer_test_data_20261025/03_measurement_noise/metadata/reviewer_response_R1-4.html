Response to Reviewer 1, Comment 4

Reviewer comment (paraphrased). Please distinguish process noise from measurement noise and clarify how measurement noise affects the history encoder and recursive prediction.

Response. We agree that these two noise sources must be separated. In the original benchmark, the CSTR simulator includes a prescribed process perturbation in the state increment during integration; it therefore changes the plant trajectory and the delayed recycle history. It is not a sensor error. The Lorenz trajectories are deterministic. Neither original benchmark adds measurement noise to the observations, so the original results did not constitute a measurement-noise robustness claim.

We added a frozen-checkpoint, paired measurement-noise audit. For each system we generated 12 new trajectories and added independent, paired perturbations only to the observed history, with amplitudes 0%, 1%, 5%, and 10% of the corresponding training-state standard deviation. The future actions, true future states, normalization scales, checkpoints, and 880 prediction origins were unchanged; no noisy future state was supplied, no re-encoding or data assimilation was performed, and no model was refitted. The audit contains 576 primary cases (six predictors, two systems, four noise levels, and 12 trajectories) plus 48 DLM--K diagnostics that perturb the past 19 samples and the current sample separately. All 624 archives are finite and the raw archives and verification files are retained.

At step 60 and 5% history noise, the DLM--K endpoint RMSE changed from 0.7731 to 0.7901 for Lorenz and from 0.007565 to 0.02943 for CSTR. The corresponding displacement from the clean-input rollout was 0.2648 and 0.02833. The encoder decomposition shows that at the first step, current-sample-only displacement was 0.04748 (Lorenz) and 0.04402 (CSTR), while perturbing the past 19 samples only gave 0.01130 and 0.003102. At step 60, past-history-only displacement was 0.1769 (Lorenz) and 0.003301 (CSTR), whereas current-sample-only displacement was 0.2162 and 0.02808. These quantities separate initial encoding sensitivity from the later recursive propagation of the perturbed latent state; they are a fixed-checkpoint sensitivity audit, not a retraining-based robustness guarantee.

We also clarified the scope of noise-aware identification. Sensor noise is an error in the observed regressors and can bias fitted linear operators. We cite Dawson et al. for sensor-noise effects and corrections in DMD and Messenger and Bortz for weak test-function formulations that can reduce derivative sensitivity under their stated assumptions. Those results do not provide an automatic guarantee for our nonlinear learned lift and AFT recurrence. A weak-form or noise-aware Koopman estimator for delayed, input-conditioned histories would require a separately specified estimator and a fitted comparison.

Changes in the manuscript. The distinction, audit protocol, quantitative results, and scope statement were added in Section 4.2, with the blue Figure 3 caption and the complete archived summary. The simulator-noise distinction was also stated in the implementation appendix. The two supporting references are blue. The seven previous revision rounds, original code/data/checkpoints, and existing bibliography entries were preserved.
