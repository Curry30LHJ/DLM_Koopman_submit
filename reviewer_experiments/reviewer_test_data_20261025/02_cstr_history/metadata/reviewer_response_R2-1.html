Reviewer 2, Comment 1

How long a history do you need and how does affect your approach compared to others?

Thank you for asking about the required history and its effect relative to the comparison methods. We have added a controlled CSTR history-length study and clarified the distinction between observed history and predicted latent memory. The new and revised manuscript text, table, figure annotations, and captions are marked in blue.

The observation window L_h counts the current state and L_h−1 preceding states, together with the preceding inputs. Its span is (L_h−1)Δt. The separate memory length M limits the buffer of recursively predicted latent states during rollout; it does not add past observations. At Δt=0.005 h and a fixed recycle delay of 0.025 h, we tested L_h=6, 20, and 40, spanning 0.025, 0.095, and 0.195 h, with M=20 throughout. Six states cover the known five-sample delay and supply the five preceding tokens needed by the length-5 patch. This is the shortest window tested with the current construction, not a universal lower bound for delayed systems. For another process, candidate windows should be assessed using validation trajectories and the relevant sampling interval and delay information.

We retrained all four neural predictors at each length, including L_h=20, on the same 50 training and 12 validation trajectories and 932 common origins per trajectory. The original seed, normalization, loss horizon, batch size, optimization budgets, learning rates, and early-stopping criteria were retained; DLM–K checkpoints were selected within the final joint stage. Each RBF reference was refitted once on the common origins with its original dictionary and regression settings. Delay–RBF retains the known five-step lag, so its input coordinates do not change across the tested window lengths. Both RBF references use shared weights across lengths, and their predictions were verified to be exactly equal. All 14 fits were frozen before generating 12 new common test trajectories, evaluated at 862 common origins per trajectory. The reported metric first takes the endpoint root-mean-square error over origins and nine standardized states within each trajectory, then averages equally over the 12 trajectories.

Table 5 and Figure 7 show that DLM–K has the lowest mean error at 17 of the 18 pre-specified length/reporting-step combinations. Its step-60 error is 0.01081, 0.007630, and 0.009331 for L_h=6, 20, and 40, respectively. Thus, 20 states give 29.4% lower error than six states, whereas 40 states give 22.3% higher error than 20 states. The neural baselines have step-60 errors between 0.06912 and 0.07620. The exception among the tabulated reporting steps is step 5 at L_h=6: Delay–RBF gives 0.01037 versus 0.01177 for DLM–K. Across the full curves, Delay–RBF is better at steps 2–5 for L_h=6 and step 2 for L_h=40; DLM–K has the lowest mean error from step 6 to step 60 for all three lengths.

These results support retaining the original 20-state observation window within the tested CSTR configurations and show that extending the window need not improve prediction. The history-dependent layers also change size: DLM–K has 46,575, 162,747, and 332,107 trainable parameters, with 1, 8, and 18 patches, respectively. Table 5 reports the corresponding counts for all four neural models. We therefore interpret the experiment as a comparison of complete predictors at different history lengths and associated capacities, not as a capacity-matched causal test of history information alone. Results use one training seed and do not establish training-seed robustness or a universally sufficient minimum history.

Manuscript changes: Section 2.1, following Equation (2), clarifies L_h and its distinction from M. Section 4.2 now includes “Sensitivity to observed history length,” Table 5, and Figure 7. The overview and primary-comparison wording were adjusted to distinguish the original benchmarks from these additional tests; the previous delay study and original benchmark tables are preserved.
