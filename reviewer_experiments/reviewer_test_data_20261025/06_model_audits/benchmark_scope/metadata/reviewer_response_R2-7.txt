Reviewer 2, Comment 7

Comment:
Why do you select those examples, the CSTR system and the Lorenz 63. Do you cover all the typical delays systems so as to generalize the conclusions of the approach?

Response:
Thank you for asking us to clarify the selection and the scope of the evidence. The two examples provide complementary autonomous and input-driven tests of the same history-conditioned, open-loop prediction procedure. We have added the selection rationale at the start of Section 4 and made the interpretation of generalization explicit in Section 4.4 and the Conclusions. These examples do not cover all typical delay-system classes.

The delayed Lorenz example has three observed states, nonlinear coupling, no exogenous input, and two coordinates evaluated at one common fixed lag. It examines whether the complete predictor can initialize from an observed history and sustain recursive prediction without subsequent observations, while reporting the resulting horizon-dependent error growth. Its autonomous setting removes future-input variation from this prediction task. Our rationale follows the specified delayed equations; it does not require a claim that every property of the original undelayed Lorenz system is retained.

The CSTR-separator example provides an input-driven process setting. Its nine states include vessel compositions and temperatures, with coupled material and energy balances, nonlinear reaction rates, and three prescribed heat inputs. The recycle-state delay represents physical transport from the separator back to the first reactor, while downstream vessel coupling remains instantaneous. This tests prediction under combined process history and known future forcing, with coordinates on different physical scales. It also connects the proposed history-based representation to a chemical-process modeling task. The heat inputs are prescribed during open-loop evaluation; this is not a closed-loop controller test.

The shared scope is a single distinct constant state delay, uniform sampling, and full-state history measurements under a fixed simulator within each condition. Both delayed Lorenz terms use the same lag, so they are not two independently varied delays. The CSTR recycle delay is a plant-state delay rather than an actuator or measurement delay. The examples do not establish performance for multiple independent lags, distributed or time-varying delays, delayed derivatives, actuator or sensor delays, irregular sampling, or partial observation. Such cases require an appropriate information representation and independent identification and validation.

We distinguish the demonstrated forms of generalization. The independent test trajectories assess prediction on previously unused trajectories generated under the stated physical coefficients, initialization, sampling, excitation, and noise prescriptions. Lorenz and CSTR use separately learned weights, so using the common construction on both demonstrates reuse of the identification and rollout procedure, without testing transfer of one frozen predictor between systems. The additional CSTR delay and history studies broaden the evidence within that process family under their stated condition-specific fitting protocols. They do not establish that a frozen model transfers to an unseen delay, plant parameter set, or observation mechanism. The finite-horizon results support the evaluated autonomous and input-driven tasks and do not constitute a class-wide accuracy or stability guarantee.
