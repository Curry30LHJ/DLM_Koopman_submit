Reviewer 1, Comment 3

This may be a rather fundamental question, but I wonder whether the presence of time delays introduces additional considerations when selecting observables for the Koopman framework. Conversely, could an appropriate choice of observables help represent or handle the delays more effectively? For example, would it be beneficial to treat individual delayed terms as separate observables and construct the lifted representation accordingly? I would appreciate the authors' insights into whether such an approach would be theoretically reasonable or practically useful.

Response:

Thank you for raising this point. Explicit delayed terms are valid candidate observables and can provide a physically interpretable dictionary when the relevant variables and lags are known. We have expanded Sections 2.1 and 3.2 to explain this construction, its relation to learned history features, and the additional conditions needed for exact finite-dimensional linear evolution.

For a deterministic retarded differential equation, the natural state is a history segment rather than the instantaneous state alone (Hale and Verduyn Lunel, 1993, pp. 38–39 and 67–68). The corresponding autonomous Koopman operator composes an observable of that segment with its solution map. Finite sampling is a separate approximation: a window covering the physical delay does not itself establish an exact closed transition from those samples.

A delayed coordinate x_i(t − ell Δt) is an evaluation functional of the history segment, provided the lag lies within that segment. This is consistent with the delay-observable constructions discussed by Kamb et al. (2020) and Pan and Duraisamy (2020). For an exactly finite-memory discrete model x_{k+1} = f(x_k, …, x_{k−q}), the full stack of current and q preceding states has a Markov update consisting of f followed by shift rows. Those shift rows are linear; f need not be. Exact evolution of a finite dictionary phi by a matrix K requires phi(G(y)) = K phi(y) on the relevant domain, i.e. invariance of its span (Brunton et al., 2016). Adding selected delayed coordinates or their nonlinear functions does not automatically satisfy this condition.

In our implementation, Delay–RBF appends the delayed Lorenz coordinates x_2(k−5), x_3(k−5), or the CSTR separator coordinates x_A3(k−5), x_B3(k−5), T_3(k−5), in the primary benchmarks. It applies a prescribed nonlinear dictionary and re-evaluates that dictionary from a rolling physical-state history during prediction. LSTM–HaKAN instead learns psi_k = E_theta(S_k), combining the sampled state–input window into predictive features without prescribing each physical lag. This map is not constrained to reconstruct the window, recover the delay, or be one-to-one. If histories with different futures produce the same initial lift under the same future inputs, the predictor cannot distinguish them. We therefore evaluate predictive sufficiency empirically rather than infer it from the architecture.

DLM–K encodes the observed history once and subsequently uses the initial lift and predicted latent states in AFT. This is distinct from adding further observed delay coordinates. We checked this information flow in both released models and verified the explicit lag selections used by Delay–RBF. The nonlinear latent-memory correction does not prove Koopman invariance. A hybrid dictionary combining known delay coordinates with learned features is a reasonable extension, but its benefits would require a separate fitted comparison; no universal superiority is asserted for either representation.

We also distinguish physical delays from lags chosen for reconstruction. The original Takens theorem concerns generic smooth diffeomorphism/observation pairs on a compact finite-dimensional manifold; it does not guarantee the chosen window length for arbitrary delay equations or linear closure (Takens, 1981, Theorem 1). For varying inputs, we explicitly retain input conditioning and discuss the input-sequence augmentation used in controlled Koopman theory (Korda and Mezić, 2018, Section 3.1). The additive B u term remains a modeling choice. These clarifications appear in Section 2.1 (PDF viewer page 4, printed page 3) and Section 3.2 (viewer page 7, printed page 6). The five added paragraphs, accompanying unnumbered shift equation, and four new reference entries are blue. All preceding six revision rounds and the original numerical results are preserved; no new training or performance experiment was conducted.
