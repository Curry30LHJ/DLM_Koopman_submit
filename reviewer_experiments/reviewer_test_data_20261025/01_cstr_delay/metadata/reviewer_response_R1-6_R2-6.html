CSTR delay experiment: draft responses to R1-6 and R2-6

Prepared from verified experiment results. This is response material for author review, not a submitted response letter. Responses are blue. The full manuscript revisions are also blue.

Reviewer 1, Comment 6

Comment (paraphrased): How does delay magnitude affect performance, and under what conditions can reliable prediction be guaranteed?

Thank you for raising the dependence on delay magnitude. We have added a paired CSTR recycle-delay experiment with delays of 0.010, 0.025, and 0.050 h, corresponding to 2, 5, and 10 sampling intervals. All six predictors were fitted separately at each delay. The physical parameters, integration and observation intervals, network structures, 20-observation history, 20-state latent-memory capacity, neural seed, optimization settings, and normalization scales were held fixed. Initial states, heat inputs, and process-noise realizations were paired across delays. Each fitted predictor was then evaluated on 12 newly generated paired test trajectories, with 882 shared prediction origins per trajectory.

DLM-K's mean error at step 60 is 0.004849, 0.006862, and 0.01066 as the delay increases. These values are 45.0%, 64.9%, and 55.7% below the corresponding Delay-RBF errors, the lowest baseline errors at this step. DLM-K gives the lowest mean error at all six pre-specified reporting steps for all three delays. The full curves also identify the early steps at which Delay-RBF has lower errors: steps 2-4 at 0.010 h and steps 2-3 at 0.050 h. Thus, the reported advantage is stated with its horizon and comparator scope.

We have also clarified what the experiment supports concerning reliable prediction. The largest tested delay, 0.050 h, lies within the 0.095 h span of the observed history. The delayed separator state is therefore present in the recorded history, but this coverage alone does not guarantee accuracy of the learned finite-dimensional lift or stability of the nonlinear memory-dependent predictor. The evidence is empirical for the tested fixed delays and horizons; a quantitative prediction guarantee is not claimed. The three conventional neural baselines improve at step 60 as the delay increases, whereas DLM-K and both RBF predictors have higher errors. We therefore describe the delay dependence as model-specific rather than assuming that every predictor must deteriorate monotonically with delay.

Changes are marked in blue in Section 4.2, paragraph 'Sensitivity to recycle delay', Table 4, Figure 6, and the first paragraph of Section 4.3 (PDF viewer pages 14-16; printed pages 13-15).

Reviewer 2, Comment 6

Comment (paraphrased): Does the delay affect the model parameters? Please verify with several delay values.

Thank you for this suggestion. We now distinguish the physical delay from the learned model parameters. In DLM-K, the delay is neither an explicit scalar input nor a scalar directly optimized by the identification procedure. It changes the trajectories from which the lifting, linear-core, and latent-memory weights are estimated. For the new experiment, these weights were therefore re-estimated separately at each of three delays, while the architecture and its 162,747 trainable parameters were retained. This tests whether the same model design can identify dynamics at several fixed delays; it does not test transfer of one frozen parameter set across delays.

The new delays are 0.010, 0.025, and 0.050 h. For each condition, all six predictors use 50 training and 12 validation trajectories, with paired initial conditions, inputs, and noise across delays. Neural training retains seed 2, batch size 256, the 30-step loss and checkpoint-selection horizon, and the original budgets, learning rates, and early-stopping rules. The final DLM-K checkpoint is selected within joint fine-tuning. Delay-RBF uses the corresponding known lag of 2, 5, or 10 samples, with its center count, kernel width, ridge coefficient, and construction seed unchanged. No hyperparameter search on the new test set was performed.

Table 4 and Figure 6 report the independent tests generated after the 18 fitted checkpoints were fixed. DLM-K gives the lowest mean error at all six pre-specified reporting steps at all three delays, with step-60 errors of 0.004849, 0.006862, and 0.01066. The separate 0.025 h result belongs to the newly generated sensitivity-test set; the original baseline results in Table 3 are retained. The learned parameter sets depend on the condition used for identification, while the chosen model size remains the same. These are single-seed fits, consistent with the original experiment; variability across training seeds is not inferred from the 12 test trajectories.

Changes are marked in blue in Section 4.2, Table 4, Figure 6, Section 4.3, the concluding paragraph, and the RBF implementation description in Appendix B.

Author note on archived horizons

The reporting horizon of 60 steps was fixed before training. All 100-step raw predictions are retained. At 0.010 h, the Markov RBF prediction on test trajectory 3 grows sharply near the end of the 100-step archive, reaching a trajectory-level endpoint error of about 1.88e107 at step 100. The 60-step mean error remains 0.1573. No predictions or trajectories were clipped, removed, or replaced. This numerical finiteness must not be described as stable behavior.
