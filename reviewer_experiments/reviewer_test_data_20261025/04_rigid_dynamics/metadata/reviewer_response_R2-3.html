Reviewer 2, Comment 3

The profile of the model may affect the performance of the method. If the dynamics is stiff at some point, how does it affect the approach?

Thank you for this question. Stiff dynamics can affect both data generation and identification: fast stable modes may restrict the stable internal step of an explicit integrator, while an observation interval that fails to resolve relevant transients can remove information needed by the learned predictor. DLM-K learns the sampled transition without integrating the governing differential equation during a forecast, but this does not remove the need for sufficiently resolved training data. We have added this explanation, numerical-resolution checks, and a distinction between plant dynamics and the complete nonlinear predictor.

The solver audit confirms that delayed Lorenz uses method-of-steps RK4 with an internal step of 1/150 and an observation interval of 1/30 in system time units. Its delay of 1/6 spans 25 internal steps and five observations, with cubic-Hermite delayed-state interpolation. A short, compatible smooth-history test over 0.2 time units at steps 1/75, 1/150, 1/300, and 1/600 gives empirical refinement orders of 4.02 and 4.04. This is an implementation and short-time convergence check; long chaotic trajectories are not required to remain pointwise close under refinement.

The CSTR simulator uses explicit Euler with an internal step of 0.001 h, observations every 0.005 h, and a recycle delay of 0.025 h. Each heat input is held for one observation interval, or five original Euler substeps. We replayed the same initial states and 1000 held input values from 12 independent holdout trajectories over 5 h using internal steps of 0.0025, 0.001, 0.0005, and 0.00025 h. Observation and delay times were retained exactly. Process noise was disabled to isolate deterministic integration error. Relative to the finest tested grid, the original step gives a mean/maximum trajectory RMSE of 0.006230/0.008642 and a mean/maximum final-state RMSE of 0.005002/0.007637 in the original training-standardized coordinates. The corresponding trajectory values at 0.0005 h are 0.002000/0.002788. All 48 replays were finite, with no clipping or numerical recovery. Table B.7(a) reports every grid. These are finite-grid discrepancies; the primary prediction errors remain defined against the specified original simulator, without an asserted error bound to an exact continuous-time plant or a stochastic convergence claim.

We also checked the delayed dynamics explicitly. For the local plant variation, both J_0(t) and J_tau(t) enter: delta x_dot(t) = J_0(t) delta x(t) + J_tau(t) delta x(t-tau). Freezing these matrices gives det[s I - J_0 - J_tau exp(-s tau)] = 0. At 100 checked states per CSTR trajectory, the current-state Jacobian has negative real parts, maximum eigenvalue magnitude 189.65 h^-1, and maximum current-mode decay-rate ratio 4.235. The original internal step satisfies h_int rho(J_0) <= 0.190. A further frozen tangent audit includes the complete recycle-history shift: at three points per trajectory, its 84-dimensional original-grid map has dominant log(rho)/h_int values from -2.310 to -1.354 h^-1. Halving the step gives -2.310 to -1.353 h^-1. The slower delayed modes show why the instantaneous decay-rate ratio does not characterize the entire delay system. These are local frozen diagnostics, not a global stability result.

For sampling resolution, we recorded 12 deterministic fine-grid trajectories per system at 20 times the observation rate, before decimation. Lorenz retains its original 26-point random histories and physical burn-in duration; CSTR retains its original initial states and held inputs. After linear detrending and Hann tapering, the maximum fraction of state power above the original Nyquist frequency is 3.527e-7 for Lorenz (Nyquist 15 per time unit) and 0.004718 for CSTR (Nyquist 100 h^-1). Maximum dominant frequencies are 2.07 per time unit and 1.60 h^-1, respectively. Table B.7(b) reports the high-frequency tails on the fine grid. This provides a descriptive sampling check; finite-window spectra do not establish strict bandlimitation, and held inputs and the original process-noise prescription are not covered by an anti-aliasing guarantee.

Finally, the selected learned core radius of approximately 0.99992 is a property of A. Plant stiffness requires the delayed physical dynamics, and predictor error propagation depends on the complete augmented latent-memory update and products of its Jacobians along the forecast. A alone establishes neither. For a strongly stiff application, a stiffness-appropriate delay solver and tolerance/step checks would be needed for data generation, followed by identification and validation at an observation interval resolving the target transients. A smaller observation interval also requires more samples and forecast steps to retain the same physical history and prediction duration. The present benchmarks do not establish performance for arbitrary strongly stiff systems.
